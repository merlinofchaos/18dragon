# 18Dragon — Introduction

*Railways in a Fantasy Land*

---

## The Year 1822 P.V.

Ask a Varstovan clerk to date a contract and she writes 1822 P.V. without thinking
about it. Post Verantum. Eighteen hundred and twenty-two years since the Empire
ended in a day.

Before that day the Empire was the world. Verantum's roads ran from the northern
harbors down to the southern sand, and any governor who wanted a decision wrote to
Verantia and waited. Then the island cracked and Verantia burned. What did it, the
histories still argue. Fire out of a clear sky, say the Caelish annals. A working of
the imperial magi that got away from them, say the magi, who would know. In Gördum
they say something old woke up under the sea and the Empire had only itself to blame
for the noise it had been making. Scholars set that year at zero and named the age
after the wound.

The provinces buried what they could and became kingdoms. Caelimor took the
northwest, a wet green country of harbors and moor, ruled out of Draeven. Gördum
shut its mountain gates and kept its forges lit, dwarven and unbothered, under
Brekheim. Varstova made a nation out of ledgers and toll roads in the eastern hills,
and Varstgrad counted the take. Muravel inherited the southern plains and the caravan
roads that cross them, which is how Kalavar got rich. Verantum itself is an island of
empty cities. Verantia is a field of broken marble; Corvium, Ostia, and Litoria stand
behind their walls with nothing inside them, and every generation produces men who
swear they can be filled again.

For eighteen centuries nothing moved faster than a horse. Then the dwarves of Gördum
went back through the mounds, the old spoil hills above the deep workings, and found
seams of phlogiston in them. It is a heavy red earth that takes fire the way stone
takes heat and gives it back for days. Gördum sold it abroad as a curiosity, mostly
to alchemists, and thought no more about it.

The College of Magi at Draeven thought a great deal about it. In 1799 they published
the phlogiston engine, a device that would turn a wheel as long as you fed it. They
were applauded. They then discovered that nothing in Draeven particularly needed a
wheel turned, and the paper sat in the College library for five years.

Dwarves read papers.

In 1804 P.V., in a shed behind the Brekheim forgeworks, dwarven craftsmen bolted a
Draeven engine to an iron frame, packed the box with Gördum phlogiston, and ran the
thing four miles up a mine track and four back with eleven tons of ore behind it.
Within ten years there was rail under every deep hall in the kingdom, and every court
above ground had an opinion about it.

This spring, Brekheim Forgeworks Rail announced service open to anyone who pays:
Brekheim out to the mountain gates, published schedule, published fare. In Draeven
the magi are still arguing over the patent, which they will lose. In Kalavar the
caravan masters have seen the fare table and understood it. And in Varstgrad, where
nobody has laid a foot of track, the houses did the thing Varstova does best. They
worked out what a railway costs.

More than any one man has. That is the whole of it. A concession is granted to a
person, and no person can pay for a railway, so it is floated. A company is
chartered, its shares are cut into tenths, and strangers put up the money for a
piece of what the trains bring back. A small line has one owner and keeps every coin
it earns. A great company has a president who paid for the seat, eight shares in
other men's hands, and a board that will put him out of it if the dividends
disappoint. And a share is not worth what it cost. It is worth what the Varstgrad
floor says this afternoon.

The floor opened to railway paper before the thaw and has not been quiet since. It
says a new number every time a company pays out, issues more paper, or fails to
reach a town it promised. Half the men shouting on it will never see a locomotive.

So: the kingdoms are granting concessions, the exchange is open, and nothing out
there is built yet. Bring your money.
