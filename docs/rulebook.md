## The Year 1822 P.V.

Ask a Varstovan clerk to date a contract and she writes 1822 P.V. without thinking
about it. Post Verantum. Eighteen hundred and twenty-two years since the Empire ended
in a day.

Before that day the Empire was the world. Verantum's roads ran from the northern
harbors down to the southern sand, and any governor who wanted a decision wrote to
Verantia and waited. Then the island cracked and Verantia burned. What did it, the
histories still argue. Fire out of a clear sky, say the Caelish annals. A working of
the imperial magi that got away from them, say the magi, who would know. In Gördum
they say something old woke up under the sea and the Empire had only itself to blame
for the noise it had been making. Scholars set that year at zero and named the age
after the wound.

The provinces buried what they could and became kingdoms. Caelimor took the northwest,
a wet green country of harbors and moor, ruled out of Draeven. Gördum shut its mountain
gates and kept its forges lit, dwarven and unbothered, under Brekheim. Varstova made a
nation out of ledgers and toll roads in the eastern hills, and Varstgrad counted the
take. Muravel inherited the southern plains and the caravan roads that cross them, which
is how Kalavar got rich. Verantum itself is an island of empty cities. Verantia is a
field of broken marble; Corvium, Ostia, and Litoria stand behind their walls with
nothing inside them, and every generation produces men who swear they can be filled
again.

For eighteen centuries nothing moved faster than a horse. Then the dwarves of Gördum
went back through the mounds, the old spoil hills above the deep workings, and found
seams of phlogiston in them. It is a heavy red earth that takes fire the way stone takes
heat and gives it back for days. Gördum sold it abroad as a curiosity, mostly to
alchemists, and thought no more about it.

The College of Magi at Draeven thought a great deal about it. In 1799 they published the
phlogiston engine, a device that would turn a wheel as long as you fed it. They were
applauded. They then discovered that nothing in Draeven particularly needed a wheel
turned, and the paper sat in the College library for five years.

Dwarves read papers.

In 1804 P.V., in a shed behind the Brekheim forgeworks, dwarven craftsmen bolted a
Draeven engine to an iron frame, packed the box with Gördum phlogiston, and ran the
thing four miles up a mine track and four back with eleven tons of ore behind it. Within
ten years there was rail under every deep hall in the kingdom, and every court above
ground had an opinion about it.

This spring, Brekheim Forgeworks Rail announced service open to anyone who pays: Brekheim
out to the mountain gates, published schedule, published fare. In Draeven the magi are
still arguing over the patent, which they will lose. In Kalavar the caravan masters have
seen the fare table and understood it. And in Varstgrad, where nobody has laid a foot of
track, the houses did the thing Varstova does best. They worked out what a railway costs.

More than any one man has. That is the whole of it. No person can pay for a railway, so a
company is chartered — its shares cut into tenths — and strangers put up the money for a
piece of what the trains bring back. A small line has one owner and keeps every coin it
earns. A great company has a president who paid for the chair and holds the largest block
of its stock — a fifth at the least, and usually a good deal more — the rest scattered
among other investors, and a board that will unseat them if the dividends disappoint. And
a share is not worth what it cost. It is worth what the Varstgrad floor says this
afternoon.

The floor opened to railway paper before the thaw and has not been quiet since. It says a
new number every time a company pays out, issues more paper, or fails to reach a town it
promised. Half the men shouting on it will never see a locomotive.

So: the charters are being drawn up, the exchange is open, and nothing out there is built
yet. Bring your money.

## How to Read This Book

18Dragon is a **variant of 1822**. This book is complete — you need no other rulebook to
play — but a reader who already knows 1822 can move quickly. Every rule that differs from
base 1822 is printed in [blue]{.delta}; rules printed in black are 1822's, unchanged. The
**Differences from 1822** appendix at the back gathers every change in one place.

Money is measured in **ducats**, written `gp` in the tables here and on the game's
components — so **150gp** means 150 ducats. Throughout, a minor company is written **Mx**
and a private company **Px**, where *x* is its number (for example M7, P28).

## Components

- The **map board** — the five regions (Caelimor, Gördum, Varstova, Muravel, and the
  Verantum island) with the round/phase track.
- The **board mat** — the stock market and the bidding boxes (4 minor + 3 private).
- **10 major company charters** and **30 minor company charters**.
- **Certificates:** for each major, one President's certificate (20%) and eight shares
  (10% each); one 50% director's certificate for each of the **30 minors**.
- **30 private company cards** — some carry a permanent or prize train (2P, LP, 5P, P+)
  on the back.
- **63 train cards** — L/2 (22, double-sided), 3 (9), 4 (6), 5 (3), 6 (3), and 7/E
  (20, double-sided).
- **Track tiles** — yellow, green, brown, and grey, including the labelled-city tiles and
  the Verantum ruins tiles.
- **Tokens** — station tokens, share-price and round markers, and four bid cubes per
  player, with their sticker sheets.
- **Player-order cards** (1–6) and a bank of money in **ducats**.
